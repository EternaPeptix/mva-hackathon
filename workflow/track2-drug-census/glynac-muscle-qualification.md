# Glycine plus N-acetylcysteine: human muscle evidence with an unresolved randomized functional contrast

**Integrated source qualification, 23 September 2026.** Retain the tested combination as a substantive human metabolic and functional-trajectory reserve. The longer randomized trial measured improved grip, gait and chair-rise trajectories during GlyNAC, but its complete published main and supplement do not provide the planned final active-versus-placebo functional estimate, confidence interval or P value. The shorter independent trial has a negative primary blood-glutathione comparison. Neither source establishes BUBR1 restoration or chromosome fidelity. This update adds one combination-specific assessment, with no original-queue or independent-study credit.

## The two trials test different preparations and populations

[Kumar et al., PMID35975308](https://doi.org/10.1093/gerona/glac135), published in the 2023 journal issue, randomized 24 older adults in Houston to pharmacy-filled capsules of **glycine 100 mg/kg/day plus NAC 100 mg/kg/day**, or alanine 200 mg/kg/day, for 16 weeks. These are doses of each ingredient, not a combined 100 mg/kg. The investigators purchased ingredients with certificates of analysis; a commercial finished-product equivalence, NAC salt and exact retail formulation were not established. A separate 12-person young cohort received GlyNAC for two weeks without randomized young controls. Older participants were 61–80 years with BMI above 27; actual means were approximately 71 years and BMI 31. The active arm had 8 men/4 women versus 4 men/8 women on placebo. Difficulty walking, diabetes, active malignancy and important untreated organ disease were excluded. This was not a pediatric or congenital-myopathy cohort. No prescribed exercise-training intervention was described.

One older active participant stopped after two weeks for a reason stated to be unrelated to the trial: Table 2 has 12 active participants at baseline and 11 at week 16, versus 12 placebo throughout. Eleven young participants appear in the tables despite 12 recruited. Pill counting and contact assessed compliance, but no numeric adherence percentage is supplied. Published ITT/per-protocol labels, a missing-data sensitivity analysis and imputation details are not explicit. A mixed model does not by itself prove an all-randomized ITT estimand.

[Lizzo et al., PMID35821844](https://doi.org/10.3389/fragi.2022.852569) is a separate German cohort: **117 older adults randomized, 114 completed**, plus 20 untreated young reference participants. Healthy, sedentary adults aged 60–85 received **2.4, 4.8 or 7.2 g/day total combination**, 1:1 glycine:NAC by mass, split twice daily for 14 days. Each ingredient therefore supplied 1.2, 2.4 or 3.6 g/day. Glanbia made the sachets; low/middle arms also contained isomaltulose to equalize total powder, and placebo supplied 7.2 g/day isomaltulose. Current registry wording names PRUVIN; this does not establish arbitrary retail-product equivalence. Unlike the Houston cohort, baseline total GSH was not lower than in young adults. These distinct dose, duration, comparator and baseline-state differences prevent treating the German trial as a replication or refutation of the Houston functional outcome.

Neither study has glycine-alone or NAC-alone arms. The combination results neither prove synergy nor transfer to either ingredient. Existing NAC appraisal **prior-28 remains unchanged**.

## The longer trial: retain the functional observations and the missing comparison

The current [NCT01870193 record](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT01870193) names muscle GSH as the primary outcome. The publication calculated sample size using red-cell GSH, not physical function. The paper explicitly added aging-hallmark analyses as exploratory work after trial completion. Registry history and a separately dated final SAP were not obtained, so the current entry does not independently prove original prespecification.

The Methods specify a separate mixed model for each outcome, cohort, time and cohort-by-time terms, and Holm correction **within each measure**. Physical-function analysis explicitly includes eight comparisons, one being older placebo versus older GlyNAC at 16 weeks. Table 2 instead prints baseline cohort comparisons, within-arm changes and comparisons with young baseline. Supplement S1 adds descriptive percentage bars but no treatment-contrast inference. The main/supplement supply neither the final active-versus-placebo functional P/CI nor the interaction result. No new inferential calculation is substituted for that omission.

No supplement-related adverse event was reported, and serial ALT/AST/creatinine comparisons were nonsignificant. Supplementary clinical labs do have other nominal changes, including two-week hemoglobin/BUN changes; do not describe every laboratory comparison as null. These small, selected, short studies do not establish pediatric or long-term safety. The Houston report declares NIH/NIA funding plus a philanthropic gift, no funder role and no conflicts; these are the source's declarations.

## The shorter trial: primary null and a mislabeled post hoc comparison

The German primary endpoint was hematocrit-normalized whole-blood total GSH after two weeks versus placebo. The three active-versus-placebo P values are **.904, .934 and .278**, with GSH-free:GSSG comparisons also nonsignificant. The source supplies arm-specific endpoint confidence intervals, not treatment-difference CIs. No clinical grip, gait, chair-rise or force outcome was measured/reported; this is **not a muscle-function null**.

The abstract's apparent “placebo 819.7 versus GlyNAC 905.4, P=.016” is not supported as a randomized contrast by the complete source. Full Results and native Figure 4C identify **819.7→905.4 as the paired baseline-to-end median change in a selected low-GSH/high-MDA subgroup receiving pooled 4.8/7.2-g doses**. The corresponding selected placebo subgroup was 824.4→780, P=.734. No between-arm change test or treatment-by-subgroup interaction is provided. Preserve this exploratory signal without converting it into a confirmed predictive subgroup or treatment effect. Selection on baseline measures, pooling and multiple post hoc analyses matter.

The prose's statement of no intra-individual GSH increase also needs qualification: Table 2 reports nominal low-dose within-arm GSH-T P=.045 and GSH-F P=.014, and placebo GSH-F:GSSG P=.040. None reverses the negative primary active-versus-placebo comparisons. There is an actual adjusted precursor signal: after excluding the highest baseline-glycine quartile, Supplement Table S6 gives baseline-adjusted glycine coefficients versus placebo of +65.10 (SE27.63), P=.021 at 4.8 g and +74.90 (SE27.29), P=.007 at 7.2 g; low-dose P=.27. This is blood glycine response, not muscle function or proof of overall GSH efficacy.

The study reports no severe adverse events and no discontinuations attributed to GlyNAC. Flow records one high-blood-pressure discontinuation; causation is not established. ALP was higher at the two larger doses versus placebo (P=.009/.010), judged clinically unimportant by the authors. Supplement Table S3 identifies the middle dose as 4.8 g, whereas safety prose says 3.6 g. Nestlé funded the study; employee, patent, licensing/marketing and Profil funding/shareholder interests are declared. These declared interests do not alter the reported randomized contrast.
