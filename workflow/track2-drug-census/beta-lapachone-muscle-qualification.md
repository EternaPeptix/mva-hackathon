# β-Lapachone: actual aged-mouse functional benefit alongside exposure-specific NAD depletion

**Retain β-lapachone as a controlled preclinical functional reserve, and refine the existing counter-probe entry without creating another compound.** The 2012 dietary study provides actual treated-versus-control motor and normalized-grip benefits. The 2018 cell study remains important counterevidence to assuming that every β-lapachone exposure expands NAD pools. Neither study establishes MVA efficacy, and the earlier BUBR1-focused discovery cannot stand as a complete compound-level functional appraisal.

## The administered dietary intervention

[Lee et al., 2012, PMID23071729](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0047122), PMC3469505, started male C57BL/6 mice on the experiment at 13 months (Results calls the strain C57BL/6J). Three dietary groups received normal chow ad libitum, 70% of normal diet as calorie restriction, or normal chow containing **0.066% β-lapachone**. Feeding continued for ten months. The authors estimated **70–80 mg/kg/day** from measured food intake, diet concentration (0.66 mg/g) and body weight. That is an intake-derived estimate, not a measured systemic or muscle concentration. Compound supplier, catalog, purity, formulation and batch characterization were not reported. The named β compound must not be replaced by α-lapachone, lapacho bark/tea, a derivative or an arbitrary product.

The study does not describe random allocation, concealment, a prespecified endpoint hierarchy, power calculation or general blinding of motor tests. A blinded observer is specifically described for fear-conditioning scoring. Initial animal counts and a complete allocation/death/censoring/assay flow are absent. Figure4 gives only **n=10–15/group**, without exact numbers for each arm and visit. Therefore those numbers cannot be treated as an unchanging full cohort or reconstructed from plotted bars.

## Functional positives and nulls

Native Figure4 supplies the following direct β-lapachone-versus-control P values. Values are mean±SEM; exact numerical treatment estimates and confidence intervals were not supplied and were not digitized.

| Endpoint | Three months of feeding | Ten months of feeding | What was measured |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Rotarod | **P=.038** | **P=.033** | Latency to fall from an accelerating rod, 2–40 rpm over five minutes; four trials with 30-minute rests. |
| Grip | P=.264 | **P=.036** | Figure4D reports maximum **forelimb grip strength (N) / body weight (g)**. The highest of five pulls was used. This is normalized grip, not an established absolute-force increase. |
| Pole test | P=.209 | **P=.003** | Number of successful trials out of five for reorienting on a vertical pole; sliding or hanging for two minutes was failure. This is not a hanging-endurance test. |

The early positive is rotarod, not all three motor measures. Calorie restriction also improves the ten-month outcomes; β-lapachone versus calorie restriction is nonsignificant then (rotarod .844, normalized grip .279, pole .776). β-lapachone's three-month rotarod comparison with calorie restriction is positive (P=.002). This does not establish overall superiority or a compound-by-time effect. Nonsignificance at three months followed by significance at ten months is not itself evidence of a changed treatment effect. Benefit is observed during continued feeding, with no withdrawal or post-treatment durability assessment.

Body weight declined earlier in the β-lapachone group, and the control survivors later lost substantial weight; Figure2 explicitly summarizes **survivors**. Fat-pad mass normalized to tibial length also decreased. Because body mass changes substantially and Figure4D uses it as a denominator, normalized grip cannot establish increased absolute grip. Multiplying group-mean normalized bars by group-mean body weight would not reconstruct the individual absolute forces. Weight/fat reduction is an observed co-effect, not evidence of growth support.

The regimen did not show a significant locomotor-distance advantage (Figure4A β-lapachone versus control P=.684) or food-intake difference (Figure2B P=.071). These are not equivalence tests. Fear-conditioning performance is positive, indicating that motor behaviour need not reflect muscle alone. No isolated muscle-force assay, muscle-specific force, BUBR1-dependent renewal or faithful chromosome-output endpoint was measured.

## Molecular observations and the NAD boundary

The acute experiment is separate: **5 mg/kg intravenously** versus vehicle in aged mice, n=5/group, increased the **muscle NAD+/NADH ratio** (native Figure1A P=.035) and AMPK-phosphorylation-associated signal (Figure1B P=.021). The plotted endpoint is a ratio; it does not disclose the individual NAD+ and NADH pools or establish a larger total pool. Acute sample timing and vehicle formulation were not specified. The body-temperature experiment used its own n=4/group and time course; those times must not be silently assigned to the NAD experiment.

The authors explicitly state that a NAD+ increase was **not directly measured in tissues from the chronically fed mice**. Their discussion sometimes speaks of increased NAD+ molecules, but Figure1A presents the ratio. A transient ratio change or faster turnover is a proposed explanation, not a demonstrated dietary NAD-pool expansion or causal mediator of the functional benefits. NQO1-knockout dependence and yeast NQR1 overexpression are cited background studies; this aged-mouse dietary experiment has no drug-by-NQO1 genetic test. No direct drug-induced SIRT2 activity or BUBR1 supply is demonstrated.

After ten months, skeletal-muscle mitochondrial number per 100 μm² is higher versus control (Figure5B P=.006, n=5 mice/group, seven images per mouse), with representative structural preservation. Image fields are not independent animals. The qPCR panel is mRNA, not protein abundance or enzyme activity: β-lapachone UCP1/UCP3 are positively marked, while its SIRT1, SIRT3, PGC1α, NRF1 and mTFA bars lack positive significance marks. Microarray overlap with calorie restriction and muscle-related transcripts is not direct protein stabilization, mitochondrial functional flux, or a BUBR1 response. The published mTFA forward/reverse primer strings are identical; this source-reporting issue further limits that exploratory transcript's interpretation without altering the behavioural comparisons.

## Survival, harms and analysis limits

Native Supplementary Figure1 shows substantial mortality and different survival curves in the same cohort used for behavioural/physiological testing, but no initial N or numbers-at-risk table. The authors report **9.1±0.2 versus 7.8±0.3 months of mean survival during the study**, described as a 17% increase, with log-rank P<.001; calorie restriction is reported as 8.6±0.2 months. This is not a demonstrated 17% extension of normal lifetime. The authors acknowledge unusually short control lifespan and possible experimental stress and say longevity needs a standard-protocol study. Censoring definitions and complete death/assay accounting are absent. Differential survival can affect which animals contribute to late functional and tissue measurements; survivor-based results cannot be generalized to the initial cohort without that information.

There is no systematic adverse-event or dedicated toxicity assessment establishing safety. Native Table1 reports selected plasma chemistry in n=5–10/group. Cholesterol and leptin have β-lapachone-versus-control significance marks, but **glucose does not**, despite prose calling it significantly lower. Unmarked GOT/GPT, LDH, creatinine and other values do not establish safety equivalence. The 2018 cellular NAD depletion likewise is not itself a measured toxicity rate.

The Methods states t/paired t tests or repeated-measures one-/two-way ANOVA with Holm–Sidak follow-up, and Kaplan–Meier/log-rank survival analysis. Exact tests are not mapped to every panel, numerical interaction effects and confidence intervals are absent, and no study-wide multiplicity correction is stated. Many molecular and behavioural outcomes, variable n, mortality and incomplete allocation/blinding details constrain the result. Three authors were company employees; public funding and the reported absence of funder involvement are disclosed. These limits do not erase the controlled functional positives.

## Reconciliation with the 2018 counterevidence

The earlier qualified [PMID29298345](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0190717) experiment administered β-lapachone at **10 μM for two hours in 16HBE cells** and found depletion of both NADH and NAD+ (n=3 measurements, replicate independence unspecified). Olaparib preserved NAD+ in combination while NADH stayed low. Increased NQO1 staining/immunoprecipitation reflects epitope accessibility under those conditions; the denaturing blot does not establish increased total NQO1. Untreated NQO1/Sirt2 colocalization is not drug-induced SIRT2 activation.

Different tissue, route, concentration, duration and experimental context mean neither observation cancels the other. The 2012 dietary study is affirmative administered-compound functional evidence. The 2018 cell study remains a warning against inferring absolute NAD-pool expansion from a redox ratio or stronger staining. Neither establishes a dose-response boundary or permits extrapolation of an effective or ineffective systemic regimen. No result from WK0202, other derivatives or unrelated human metabolomics is imported.

The appropriate competitive consequence is a **preclinical muscle/motor-function reserve with unresolved NAD mechanism**, retaining exposure-specific counterevidence. It merits comparison with niacin's unproven restoration hypothesis and human engagement/open-label strength context. The aged-mouse functional contrast is real; it does not prove MVA superiority, equivalence to pediatric disease evidence, or clinical eligibility. No selective BUBR1-only threshold is imposed, and no automatic nominee change follows.
