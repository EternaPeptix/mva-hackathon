# L-leucine: mixed human muscle-function evidence

23 September 2026 (Arizona; retrieval timestamps extend into 24 September UTC). Integrated comparison of two older-adult trials. The 225-entry baseline had no leucine appraisal; this update adds one while preserving every previous assessment.

**Retain L-leucine as a mixed functional comparator, with lower confidence than the smaller trial's abstract suggests.** The 2020 trial reports actual walking and expiratory-pressure benefits relative to placebo; these should survive the census as functional evidence. ## The smaller trial: a real contrast, chiefly preservation

[Martínez-Arnau et al., 2020, PMID32230954/PMC7230494](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7230494/) randomized 50 nursing-home residents aged 65–93. Native Figure 1 assigns 28 to L-leucine and 22 to lactose; after five and three withdrawals, **23 and 19 were analyzed**. The authors acknowledge that this is not intention-to-treat. Randomization was performed by two supplier-employed coauthors, who reportedly did not see participants or results; a reproducible sequence/concealment algorithm is not described. Company funding and product supply are disclosed.

Bonusan supplied **L-leucine powder, 6 g/day**, approximately 3 g after breakfast and 3 g in the afternoon, dissolved in water or juice under nurse supervision for 13 weeks. Lactose was the 6-g/day control. Purity, catalog number and batch certificate are not given. Participants maintained usual diets/activity. Baseline assessment preceded randomization by about five weeks; final assessment occurred within a month after treatment ended.

Native Figure 2 and Results establish these **between-arm baseline-relative comparisons** (100 denotes baseline, not a 100% increase):

| Outcome | Leucine versus placebo | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Walking time | 101.43% versus 134.41%, P=.011 | Less slowing relative to baseline, principally placebo deterioration. This is not a demonstrated large improvement above leucine's own baseline. |
| Maximum expiratory mouth pressure (MEP) | 99.05% versus 77.67%, P=.026 | Relative preservation of measured respiratory pressure; not direct diaphragm force or fewer respiratory complications. |
| Handgrip strength | P=.437 | No significant baseline-relative treatment difference. |
| Skeletal muscle-mass index | P=.168 | No significant treatment difference; abstract/discussion mass-benefit wording overstates this comparison. |
| Inspiratory mouth pressure / peak flow | P=.105 / .272 | Nonsignificant comparisons. |

Absolute follow-up walking-time and MEP comparisons were nonsignificant (P=.285/.875), a different analysis from the positive relative-change contrasts. These are not interchangeable. Mann–Whitney testing at .05 is described without a multiplicity correction. No individual data or independent analysis verifies the reported large standardized effects. Missingness, baseline imbalance in muscle-mass index and potentially unstable individual percentage changes remain relevant. General daily function, cognition and nutritional assessments did not show benefit.

Reporting limits are concrete: completion is printed as 42, 86%, and 14 men plus 29 women—mutually inconsistent; the native flowchart supports 42/50, or 84%. A handgrip baseline paragraph gives values inconsistent with Table 1. The power paragraph calls a parallel trial a crossover. Do not repair these values silently. The current [NCT03831399 registry](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03831399) lists 59 participants and eligibility from age 60, and classifies respiratory strength as **secondary**, whereas the paper groups it with primary outcomes. Registry text also differs over placebo composition and dosing times. Its current version is post-publication, so original prespecification is not established by this comparison. No posted results or protocol document is available there.

## LACE: larger, longer and overall negative

[Witham/LACE study group, 2022, PMID35174663/PMC8977979](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8977979/) randomized 145 adults aged ≥70, versus the planned 440; funding stopped recruitment because it was slow. Four factorial arms were independently allocated perindopril/placebo and leucine/placebo. Table 1 gives **72 leucine versus 73 no-leucine**; the abstract's 72/72 is inconsistent. There was no treatment-interaction evidence (P=.59), allowing the planned separate leucine comparison, not an inseparable combination effect.

Amino GmbH supplied bulk **leucine powder**, quality-released by Tayside Pharmaceuticals: **2.5 g with meals three times daily (7.5 g/day)** for one year, versus lactose powder. It could be mixed with drinks, yoghurt or food. The read LACE documents do not supply an explicit L-isomer string, CAS, purity or batch certificate; preserve their source label rather than inventing formulation details. Median adherence was approximately 76% in both arms. Allocation was remote and concealed; outcome assessors were masked. Modified intention-to-treat repeated-measures models used available six- and twelve-month data; twelve-month SPPB was available for 52/72 and 57/73 participants.

The primary adjusted SPPB difference was **0.1 point, 95% CI −1.0 to 1.1, P=.90**. This interval includes clinically meaningful improvement and worsening; under-recruitment prevents an equivalence claim. Twelve-month-only and missing-as-zero sensitivity analyses were also null. Grip strength, quadriceps strength, gait speed, six-minute walk, chair stands, daily activities and muscle mass showed no significant benefit. A nominal worse EQ-5D utility result (−.06, CI −.11 to −.01, P=.03) occurred among many unadjusted tests; it is not general supplement harm. Supplement Table S4 records 187 versus 196 adverse events and 67/72 versus 64/73 people with an event after pooling the factorial cells; event counts and affected people are different denominators.

Positive subgroup estimates must also remain visible: low baseline protein intake had +2.6 SPPB points [0.6,4.5], and a post hoc confirmed-sarcopenia subgroup had +1.7 [0.7,2.7]. The age-over-80 subgroup also had +1.3 points [0.4,2.3]. Reported interaction P values were .70, .06 and .76, respectively; these do not establish a responsive subgroup. These are the published interaction values, not independently recomputed statistics. No multiplicity correction was applied. The [published protocol](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5753568/) supports the primary functional question, but its visit schedule differs from the final report. The current [ISRCTN90094835 record](https://www.isrctn.com/ISRCTN90094835), retrieved through its documented public XML API, labels twelve-month SPPB primary; the journal emphasizes repeated follow-up. Both reported analyses are null. No registry-history or final statistical-plan audit is claimed.

## Decision and identity boundaries

Neither main reports a BUB1B/BubR1 response, chromosome-fidelity assay or MVA cohort. Leucine's mTOR/protein-synthesis rationale is background, not measured causal mediation in these trials. Its actual human functional signals make it a reasonable common-molecule comparator without demanding MVA proof that the nominees lack. The evidence does not currently justify replacing niacin's distinct restoration hypothesis or apitegromab's stronger disease-specific motor-function comparison. This is an evidential ranking, not a patient regimen or a claim of comparative MVA efficacy.

The entry uses **L-leucine** (exact catalog relation CHEMBL291962; NIH groups 2188/2315). Name links do not establish current availability, approval or equivalent efficacy for protein powders, branched-chain mixtures, D-leucine, isoleucine or leucine metabolites. Fresh exact-source metadata/searches identified no correction/retraction notice; this bounded check is not exhaustive. The most useful unresolved work is source-data clarification of the smaller study and the final LACE analysis plan, rather than promotion from nominal subgroup significance.

Creatine remains a more directly relevant pediatric neuromuscular strength comparator, with its own mixed outcomes.
