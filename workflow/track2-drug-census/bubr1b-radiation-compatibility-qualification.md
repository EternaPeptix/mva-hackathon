# BUB1B and radiation response in breast cancer: a genetic safety clue

**Targeted primary-source follow-up, 29 September 2026.** Luo et al.,
[*Oncology Reports* 54:147 (2025)](https://www.spandidos-publications.com/10.3892/or.2025.8980/download),
studied BUB1B knockdown in breast cancer cells. This is not a drug or dietary
compound experiment and does not add a candidate to the repurposing list. It
does add a specific oncology compatibility question for proposals that could
increase functional BUBR1.

## What the study measured

The authors selected MDA-MB-231 cells, which had the highest BUB1B expression
among the breast-cancer lines they screened, and established stable shRNA
knockdown with a non-targeting control. BUB1B knockdown reduced cell growth,
colony formation and EdU incorporation. In an **unirradiated** xenograft
experiment, tumors from BUB1B-knockdown cells grew less than control tumors
(five mice per group as shown in the figure caption).

For the radiation-related experiments, MDA-MB-231 cells received **8 Gy**
ionizing radiation at 1 Gy/min for the γ-H2AX assay; the neutral comet assay was
performed two hours after 8 Gy. Compared with control cells, shBUB1B cells
showed less irradiation-associated G2/M accumulation, higher γ-H2AX signal,
greater comet tail moment and lower RAD51 protein. The study therefore supports
a role for BUB1B in the measured cell-cycle and DNA-repair responses after
radiation.

## Limits for the MVA drug comparison

This is a genetic loss-of-function experiment in one breast-cancer line. It did
not test niacin, NMN, sodium butyrate or another administered compound; did not
increase BUB1B; did not measure chromosome segregation fidelity; and did not
test an irradiated xenograft or clonogenic radiation survival. The breast
cancer line is not rhabdomyosarcoma, patient-derived tumor, skeletal muscle or
an MVA model. A change in DNA-damage markers is not itself a measured clinical
radiotherapy response.

It is reasonable to infer that a BUBR1-restoring intervention could alter
radiation responses in a tumor if it also changes tumor-cell BUBR1. This paper
does **not** show that such an intervention causes radioresistance or worsens
cancer outcomes. The inference supports testing the candidate alongside the
actual oncology regimen when radiotherapy is part of care, with tumor-cell
survival and nonmalignant muscle function assessed separately. It does not
justify an antitumor claim or a clinical treatment change.

This source strengthens an existing requirement for tumor-compatibility
testing; it does not change the niacin/NMN restoration comparison or the
apitegromab compensation role. No new drug appraisal or efficacy estimate is
added. Reading was limited to the publisher's primary article sections on
models, knockdown, radiation assays, relevant results and figures; no raw data
were reanalyzed.
