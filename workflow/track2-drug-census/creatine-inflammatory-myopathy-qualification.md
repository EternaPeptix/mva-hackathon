# Creatine with exercise in adult inflammatory myopathy and aging

The 2007 polymyositis/dermatomyositis trial provides a **randomized functional-performance benefit** when creatine was added to shared home exercise in clinically weak adults receiving stable conventional treatment. Its main-text ITT analysis reports median AFPT changes of −11.4% versus −3.7%, P = .029, although the abstract gives different magnitudes. Selected secondary proximal-strength findings were also positive. The 2016 older-adult trial instead found **greater DXA lean-mass gain without demonstrated additional strength benefit** over shared resistance training. Neither result should erase the other or be generalized to MVA.

The source producer read both complete scientific mains and all native outcome tables/figures. Independent primary review accepted the qualification after reading both complete mains and eleven selected native pages. This follow-up refines the existing `new-creatine-monohydrate-muscle` comparison, preserving its [earlier DMD evidence](creatine-muscle-qualification.md), [FSHD evidence](creatine-fshd-qualification.md), rationale and actual source depth. ## Chung 2007: adult inflammatory myopathy

[Chung et al., Arthritis & Rheumatism 2007;57:694–702](https://doi.org/10.1002/art.22687), PMID **17471547**, was a six-month randomized, double-blind trial in UK and Swedish rheumatology services. The authentic [nine-page primary PDF](https://www.myositis.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/02/Alexanderson2007.pdf) is publicly hosted by The Myositis Association; its identity matches the publication metadata.

Participants had polymyositis or dermatomyositis for more than six months, low stable disease activity but continuing weakness, and enough mobility to exercise. Prior systemic steroid treatment had lasted at least six months; current prednisolone was stable for two months and immunosuppressants for three months where used. Inclusion-body myositis and serious renal/liver disease were excluded. There were 19 creatine and 18 placebo participants, predominantly women; mean ages were 59 and 50 years. Polymyositis/dermatomyositis counts were 10/9 with creatine and 12/6 with placebo. These are chronically treated adult inflammatory diseases, distinct from pediatric dystrophinopathy, FSHD and healthy aging.

The preparation was **highly purified creatine monohydrate from Flamma Ltd, Italy**, given orally at 20 g/day for eight days, then 3 g/day for the remaining six-month study. Matching placebo followed the same mass schedule, came from the same manufacturer, and contained lactose plus other inert constituents. Full excipients, calorie matching and batch identity were not reported. Both groups were prescribed home exercise five days weekly: resistance exercises for upper/lower limbs, neck and trunk, range-of-motion work, stretching and a 15-minute walk. Adherence was assessed by interview rather than objective training-volume records. This is an add-on comparison under shared exercise, not proof of creatine–exercise synergy or efficacy without exercise.

Hospital pharmacies generated separate random-number lists for the two centers, without stratification, and supplied matching containers. Allocation remained concealed through statistical analysis. Of 60 screened, 23 were excluded and 37 randomized; **29 completed and eight withdrew**—one for surgery, six for transport difficulties and one for personal reasons. The flowchart does not give completer counts by treatment arm. The ITT analysis used **last observation carried forward (LOCF)**; completer analyses are separate and do not eliminate missing-data assumptions.

The primary endpoint, **aggregate functional performance time (AFPT)**, sums four timed tasks: a 50-foot walk, get-up-and-go including a 50-foot walk, and ascent/descent of 19 stairs. Lower time means better performance. This objective lower-limb functional measure was not fully validated specifically for inflammatory myopathy, and joint problems can affect it. It is still functional evidence, not a biomarker proxy.

These are reported Mann–Whitney comparisons of percentage changes; no confidence interval for the treatment contrast is supplied. The abstract's estimates resemble rounded completer values, while Figure 2A's caption calls its graph ITT. **The discrepancy is preserved, not resolved by relabeling participants or extracting a new estimate from pixels.** The positive randomized comparison remains, but its precise reported magnitude requires qualification. At three months, the between-arm comparison was not significant despite within-arm improvement in both groups.

The key secondary functional index (maximum 64) improved within both groups. Its six-month **ITT between-group comparison was nonsignificant**; a completer comparison of endpoint scores favored creatine, P = .030. Between-group percentage-change comparisons were nonsignificant in both analyses. A ceiling effect was acknowledged. Native Table 2 gives the creatine ITT within-arm P as .012, whereas the prose uses .034, which the table assigns to completers.

Native Table 3 preserves actual secondary strength findings: bilateral shoulder abduction and hip flexion favored creatine at three and six months. Six-month P values were below .05 for shoulder abduction and below .01 for hip flexion. Elbow-flexion and knee-extension differences at three months were not sustained at six months; ankle dorsiflexion comparisons were nonsignificant. These are nominal secondary ordinal-score comparisons across multiple muscle groups and timepoints; an adjusted change-score treatment estimate and multiplicity correction are not reported. A native table quartile of 5.50 also exceeds the described 0–5 scale and is not silently corrected.

Pain, health status, mood and fatigue questionnaires did not show treatment differences. CK and other laboratory measures did not establish modification of the underlying inflammatory disease. The authors explicitly discuss that distinction.

The MRS subset comprised **14 creatine and 10 placebo participants from the UK**. Creatine-arm PCr/β-NTP mean ratios were 4.83 at baseline, 5.07 at three months and 4.98 at six months; placebo ratios were 4.03, 3.96 and 4.05. The reported creatine-arm increases were 5.4% at three months (paired P = .06) and 3.4% at six months (paired P = .05); the abstract uses P < .05. Pi/β-NTP did not change in either group. These are **separate within-arm tests**, with different baseline ratios—not a demonstrated between-treatment metabolite effect or mediation of functional benefit. The five-person uncontrolled pilot is hypothesis-generating context, not another efficacy trial.

There were **22 adverse events in 13 participants**, described as six to seven per group: eight infections, six falls, two gastrointestinal events and other events not individually detailed. Authors considered none related to creatine or other myositis medication. Thus “no clinically relevant creatine-associated adverse events” does not mean no adverse events. ESR, CK and creatinine showed no significant changes within or between groups; this small selected cohort does not establish unrestricted renal or MVA safety.

The initial sample-size reasoning used pilot within-group strength variability; the planned 56 participants were not recruited, and the reported 75% power was post hoc. No registration identifier, original protocol or SAP was found in the main; an exact PMID/DOI ClinicalTrials.gov query returned no records. That bounded lookup does not prove absence from every registry. Public and charitable funders are listed, including the Myositis Support Group, Arthritis Research Campaign, MRC and Swedish foundations. Flamma is identified as supplier; free supply or funding is not assumed. No explicit author conflict declaration was located in the main.

## Pinto 2016: older adults receiving resistance training

[Pinto et al., Journal of Cachexia, Sarcopenia and Muscle 2016;7:413–421](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4864174/), PMID **27239423**, DOI **10.1002/jcsm.12094**, compared creatine plus resistance training with maltodextrin plus the same training for 12 weeks in Brazil.

The study screened 42, randomized **32—16 per arm—and analyzed 27 completers: 13 creatine and 14 placebo**. Five discontinued for personal reasons. No ITT imputation is reported. The Methods overview uses final 13/14 counts when describing assignment, but Results and native Figure 1 establish the randomized 16/16 denominators. Participants were nonathletic men and women aged 60–80, with mean ages about 67. Renal, hepatic and cardiac disease were excluded; some used stable antihypertensive, diabetes, thyroid or hormone medications. The paper's broader cohort should not be treated as uniformly sarcopenic.

The preparation was **5 g/day creatine monohydrate powder from MEDNUTRITION INC., Brazil**; a tested sample was 99.7% pure by HPLC. The comparator was 5 g/day maltodextrin from the same supplier. Both groups trained three times weekly for roughly 60 minutes, using supervised progressive resistance exercise after familiarization. Adherence was 89.1% versus 92.9%, P = .274. Coded packages supported blinding; correct supplement guesses did not differ significantly, but this does not prove perfect masking. The random sequence-generation procedure is not detailed.

On training days, the paper describes the assigned packages dissolved in a beverage containing **100 g maltodextrin** after exercise. The registry instead explicitly describes placebo as 100 g maltodextrin total on training days. Whether the placebo package added a further 5 g is unresolved; neither 100 g nor 105 g should be silently selected as the actual control exposure. On other days, the stated comparison was 5 g creatine versus 5 g maltodextrin. Exact calorie matching is not established. A training-related improvement in both groups does not prove a creatine-specific or synergistic effect.

Values follow native Table 3; the abstract rounds the lean-mass P to .02. Delta scores were compared with a t-test or Mann–Whitney test according to distribution. No treatment-effect confidence intervals or multiple-outcome adjustment are reported. The 80% achieved-power statement was retrospective. Both groups gained strength within arm, but this does not supply an added creatine effect; prose gives P < .0001 for those changes while the table prints .001. The placebo leg-press SD is 33.4 in prose and 33.5 in the table.

Other between-arm body-composition, skeletal-muscle-index and bone-density/content comparisons were nonsignificant. Changes in sarcopenia categories were descriptive and untested, not proof of clinical reversal. DXA lean soft tissue does not establish muscle-fibre regeneration or distinguish hydration from contractile tissue. Muscle creatine/PCr was not measured. The lean-mass result must be credited as such without upgrading it to strength or functional recovery.

Participants reported no creatine-induced side effects, but both groups reported exercise-related pain, limb swelling and restricted range of motion. Arm-specific event counts and severity were not supplied, and renal laboratory outcomes were not described. These observations do not establish universal safety. FAPEG support and MedNutrition supplement supply were acknowledged; the authors declared no conflicts of interest.

The official [RBR-7r7t52 registry page](https://ensaiosclinicos.gov.br/rg/RBR-7r7t52), retained through the web reader after direct access returned 403, gives registration on **2 March 2015**, after the paper's June–November 2014 study period, and first enrollment on 4 July 2014. The represented page's last approval is 15 June 2016 and includes outcome results. It specifies 28 planned participants and diagnosed sarcopenia, unlike the paper's 32 randomized broader cohort. It also describes pre/post mass and strength thresholds, which must not replace the randomized between-arm comparisons. Historical field versions, an original protocol and SAP were not read; prospective prespecification is not established from this retrospectively registered representation. The paper and registry also list differing ethics identifiers.

## Scope for a later creatine refinement

The adult inflammatory-myopathy functional positive deserves fair comparison on direct function, relevant exposure, duration, missingness and safety—without imposing a BUBR1 requirement on compensation evidence or favoring an incumbent by default. The aging lean-mass positive and strength nulls belong alongside it. Stable immunosuppression, home exercise and older-adult supervised training are distinct contexts and must not be pooled with earlier pediatric DMD or FSHD studies.

Neither trial establishes BUBR1 restoration, chromosome-segregation fidelity, healthy-tissue renewal, genotype rescue, MVA efficacy or superiority over another candidate. Restoration claims would additionally require relevant mechanistic evidence. Exact source, independent-review and reading-manifest bindings are in the companion JSON.
