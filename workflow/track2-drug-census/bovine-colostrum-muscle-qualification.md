# Bovine colostrum as a common-compound muscle-function challenger

**Disposition: retain as an appendix-level nutritional challenger, not a Track 2 nominee.** One small randomized older-adult study reported a lower-body strength advantage for a specific bovine-colostrum powder during supervised training. Other measured strength, muscle-size, lean-mass, and growth-factor outcomes did not show a product-specific benefit, and related trials report different outcome patterns. No study measured BUB1B/BUBR1 or MVA.

This is a source-bound, single-reader qualification outside the curated decision ledger. It adds no original-queue reading credit. Independent review remains pending.

## The older-adult trial

Duff et al. (PMID 24281841; NCT01792297) randomized 40 adults over age 50, with group mean ages near 59 years, to eight weeks of supervised resistance training plus either 60 g/day bovine colostrum or 60 g/day whey protein complex. Both powders supplied about 38 g protein per day, and both groups trained three nonconsecutive days per week.

The intervention was a product-specific preparation: Eterna Gold, heat-treated spray-dried colostrum reported as greater than 25% IgG. The full accepted author manuscript appears in the first author’s University of Saskatchewan dissertation and states that it was reprinted by permission; the publisher’s final article was not accessible during this review. The manuscript identity and results were cross-checked against PubMed and the official trial record. [University repository manuscript](https://harvest.usask.ca/handle/10388/ETD-2015-12-2326), [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24281841/), [NCT01792297](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT01792297).

The leg-press 1RM result was positive: mean changes were +24 ± 29 kg with colostrum versus +8 ± 16 kg with whey; the group-by-time test was P=.026. An analysis of covariance adjusting for baseline strength gave post-intervention means of 165 ± 5 versus 149 ± 5 kg (P=.045). This was one of three registry-listed primary outcomes. Lean tissue mass and bench-press strength were not better with colostrum than whey; neither were knee-extensor or elbow-flexor thickness, serum IGF-1, or CRP. Urinary N-telopeptides favored colostrum, but this is a bone-resorption marker, not a fracture or bone-density benefit.

The authors cautioned that the leg-press result could be a type I error among multiple tests. The sample-size calculation was based on lean-mass estimates from younger adults, not a leg-press treatment effect, and no multiplicity adjustment was identified. The current registry was first submitted after the study had started; pre-enrollment outcome prespecification is therefore not established. The registry records actual enrollment of 40 and three primary outcomes, but has no posted results section.

The manuscript says analyses were intention-to-treat, but one whey participant was lost to follow-up and its missing-data method is not described. Two other whey participants stopped the supplement after reflux but continued training. Supplement compliance was 97 ± 12% for colostrum and 88 ± 23% with whey. Two colostrum participants reported mild gastrointestinal events; three whey participants reported GI events, including two who discontinued the supplement. Funding included Mitacs and the product manufacturer, Saskatoon Colostrum Company.

## Other human trials and product boundaries

Earlier studies do not establish a consistent general muscle-strength effect. A 2003 trial in trained men found higher jump and cycle peak power with 60 g/day colostrum, but no between-group benefit in 1RM or alactic work capacity, and no IGF-1 rise. A 2001 active-adult study reported no difference in measured exercise performance. A 2004 trial found no advantage over whey for 1RM or maximal voluntary torque. A four-arm resistance-training trial of protein, colostrum, creatine, and colostrum-plus-creatine reported no between-group differences in strength, endurance, or sprint capacity. These are separate populations, products, and designs; combination results are not assigned to colostrum alone.

A 2025 randomized rugby-player study reported a vertical-jump change of +1.0 cm with colostrum versus -0.5 cm with soy protein (P=.0023), but no between-group effect on upper- or lower-body strength, aerobic power, or body composition. It is an abstract-level result in trained athletes, not an independent test of the Eterna Gold preparation. [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40640599/).

## Relevance and competitive position

Bovine colostrum is a complex food-derived preparation, not a single molecule. The older-adult trial’s improvement could be a product-specific training response; it does not identify an active component. Serum IGF-1 did not increase, so the trial does not establish an IGF-mediated mechanism. None of the reviewed studies measured BUB1B/BUBR1, checkpoint function, aneuploidy, chromosome fidelity, patient-derived MVA models, or cancer-related outcomes.

Against the current leaders, colostrum offers one controlled leg-press signal but no proximal BUBR1 rationale. Niacin retains the more gene-proximal restoration hypothesis; apitegromab has stronger pediatric neuromuscular-disease motor-function evidence, albeit in SMA rather than MVA; and the repository’s creatine evidence spans more neuromuscular and myopathy contexts. These distinct interventions cannot be ranked by cross-trial effect size. **Keep colostrum in the appendix as a product-specific oral compensation hypothesis, not a main nominee or an MVA treatment claim.**

Reconsider only after an independent, prospectively specified replication with the exact product, a protein- and energy-matched comparator, complete randomized accounting, and a prespecified functional endpoint. Any relevant BUB1B model should test absolute output together with renewal and chromosome fidelity; no BUBR1-restoration claim is justified without direct BUBR1 measurements.
