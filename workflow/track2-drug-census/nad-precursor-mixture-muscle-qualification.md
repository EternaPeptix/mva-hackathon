# Low-dose NAD-precursor mixture as a niacin counterexample

**Disposition: retain as bounded counterevidence to generic NAD-precursor claims; do not treat it as a niacin efficacy test or a Track 2 candidate.** A short randomized crossover in physically compromised older adults found no increase in skeletal-muscle NAD+ and no improvement in objective muscle/mitochondrial function after a low-dose mixture of L-tryptophan, nicotinic acid and nicotinamide. It measured no BUB1B/BUBR1 outcome, chromosome fidelity or MVA response.

## Trial and treatment contrast

Connell et al. (PMID 34191033; DOI 10.1093/jn/nxab193; NCT03310034) reported a randomized, double-blind, controlled crossover study. The registry records 14 actual participants; the paper describes 13 participants, with outcome-specific sample sizes of 11–13. Participants were community-dwelling adults aged at least 65 with impaired physical function, not people selected for NAD deficiency or a neuromuscular diagnosis. Each treatment period lasted 32 days with at least five weeks of washout.

The intervention supplied a reported total of 207.5 mg/day niacin equivalents: 210 mg/day L-tryptophan, 4 mg/day nicotinic acid and 200 mg/day nicotinamide. The control was an amino-acid mixture resembling the whey-protein source but without those three precursors. The intervention therefore cannot isolate nicotinic acid, and its 4 mg/day nicotinic-acid dose is far below the 750–1,000 mg/day niacin exposure in Pirinen's open-label mitochondrial-myopathy study.

## Findings

- Skeletal-muscle NAD+ did not differ between intervention and control (P=.123); NAAD also did not differ (P=.262; n=11). Skeletal-muscle methyl-nicotinamide increased (P=.001), consistent with altered NAD turnover but not proof of greater NAD+ availability or BUBR1 engagement.
- The reported primary mitochondrial-respiration comparisons were nonpositive, including ADP-stimulated respiration (P=.716) and maximal uncoupled respiration (P=.495). Net exercise efficiency was also nonpositive (P=.636 in the Results; the abstract reports P=.342 for its stated comparison, a discrepancy not resolved here). The report preserves the main-text statistic and flags the abstract/main-text mismatch rather than selecting one silently.
- SPPB balance, gait, chair-rise and total scores did not improve. One reported RAND-36 perceived-physical-function domain favored intervention (P=.031; n=12); other reported domains were nonpositive. The authors caution that it was one domain among multiple questionnaire domains, and no multiplicity correction was identified. Figure 6 describes eight domains while the Discussion says one of nine, another reporting inconsistency.
- No difference was reported in body composition, muscle volume, cycling energy expenditure or exercise efficiency. Compliance was reported as 100% for intervention sachets and 99% for control. One participant reported a cold sore that resolved without intervention; the 32-day trial is too small and short to establish broader safety.

The sample-size calculation was for primary mitochondrial respiration, not for the functional secondary outcomes. The registry was submitted and posted before the stated study start; it currently lists the study as terminated after the COVID-19 outbreak and contains no posted results. The article says all other outcomes beyond its primary and secondary measures were exploratory. One coauthor was affiliated with Danone Research and another with Friesland Campina; the paper reports no conflicts and lists TIFN support. These details are reported as context, not evidence of bias.

## Competitive interpretation

This study lowers confidence in the broad claim that any NAD precursor, at any dose, will raise human-muscle NAD+ or improve function. It does **not** show that high-dose niacin monotherapy fails: nicotinic acid was only 4 mg/day, two other precursors were co-administered, and no BUBR1-deficient population was studied. Its results strengthen the need to distinguish nicotinic acid from nicotinamide, NMN, NR and precursor mixtures rather than pooling them as interchangeable interventions.

Relative to the current leaders, the mixture has no direct BUB1B/BUBR1 rationale or objective functional positive. Niacin retains the more proximal but still unproven NAD/SIRT2/BUBR1 hypothesis; apitegromab retains stronger pediatric motor-function evidence in SMA, without MVA transfer. **Keep this paper in niacin's counterevidence, not as a third nominee and not as a reason to change the provisional pair.**
