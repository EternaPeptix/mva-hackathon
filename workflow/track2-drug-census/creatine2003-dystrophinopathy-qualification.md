# Creatine monohydrate in the 2003 DMD/BMD crossover

Louis and colleagues reported favorable placebo-versus-creatine comparisons for elbow strength, fatigue resistance and joint stiffness in a small crossover study of 15 boys with dystrophinopathy. The full paper’s Table 2 explicitly labels these comparisons; the abstract’s separate treatment-period descriptions should not be the sole basis for interpreting the trial. However, improvements persisted through the two-month washout, and the paper does not report a model accounting for period, sequence or carryover. These findings support further consideration of the tested preparation as a functional-compensation comparator, with substantial uncertainty about effect size and causal precision. They establish neither MVA benefit nor BUBR1 restoration. [Primary paper](https://doi.org/10.1002/mus.10355); [lawful university-hosted main](https://research.dial.uclouvain.be/bitstreams/f3892f12-979c-401d-a5a9-65a52901114f/download).

## Preparation, participants and design

The intervention was oral creatine **monohydrate supplied by Flamma, Italy**, 3 g daily for three months, compared with maltodextrin placebo. The abstract describes 3 g of creatine or maltodextrin daily; the Methods explicitly give the creatine dose but do not independently detail placebo mass, energy matching, vehicle, packaging, purity, batch, masking procedures or a retail formulation. Oral absorption is supported by the reported increases in plasma and urinary creatine. These measurements do not establish adequate exposure in every relevant muscle compartment or equivalence to current products. Shared supplier naming with another Flamma trial does not establish batch or formulation equivalence.

Fifteen boys participated: 12 with Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) and three with Becker muscular dystrophy (BMD). The stated age range is 6–16 years (10.8 ± 2.8 years); Table 1 includes a 16.1-year-old participant. Diagnosis required clinical examination and dystrophin mutation identification after muscle biopsy. Boys receiving another drug therapy or unable to generate at least 1 N·m of elbow-flexion torque were excluded. This selection limits transfer to contemporary steroid-treated DMD and to more severe weakness. Ten were wheelchair dependent and five were not; the latter subgroup included both diagnoses. These are not 15 DMD participants or two independent trials.

Eight boys received creatine first and seven placebo first. After the first three-month period, a two-month washout preceded crossover to the other treatment for three months. The study is described as randomized and double blind, but sequence generation, allocation concealment and practical masking are not explained. No assigned exercise intervention is described. The paper reports testing before and after each period. Table 2 specifies n=15 except the n=6 stiffness and spectroscopy outcomes; it supplies no participant-flow diagram, explicit missing-data method, ITT definition or separate complete-pair accounting. The six spectroscopy participants were the slimmest boys able to fit into the scanner. The reason for the separate stiffness n=6 is not explained, and the two subsets should not be assumed identical.

## Functional results and the actual comparisons

Elbow-flexion maximal voluntary contraction (MVC) was measured isometrically, retaining the best of three efforts. Fatigue resistance was time maintaining 75% of MVC after an eight-minute recovery. Joint stiffness was measured with mechanical perturbations at 25% and 50% MVC; the reported outcome table gives only 50% MVC. These are measured muscle-performance outcomes, distinct from walking, daily activity, bone density and biomarkers.

The following values are Table 2 means ± SE, with its explicit **“Placebo vs Cr”** P-value column. No treatment-effect confidence interval or independently specified adjusted treatment estimate is given.

| Outcome | Placebo, pre → post | Creatine, pre → post | Reported comparison |
|---|---|---|---|
| MVC, N·m; n=15 per table footnote | 5.01 ± 1.5 → 4.9 ± 1.6 | 5.4 ± 2.1 → 6.1 ± 2.2 | P=.02 |
| Time to exhaustion, seconds; n=15 per footnote | 4.9 ± 1.8 → 4.2 ± 1.2 | 4.1 ± 1.3 → 7.2 ± 1.4 | P<.001 |
| Total joint stiffness at 50% MVC, N·m·rad⁻¹; n=6 | 17.7 ± 4.0 → 21.9 ± 4.5 | 19.8 ± 6.1 → 19.3 ± 4.7 | P=.03 |
| Calf PCr/β-ATP; n=6 | 3.8 ± 0.2 → 3.9 ± 0.2 | 3.6 ± 0.3 → 3.9 ± 0.2 | P=.04; distinct nonsignificant wording in text |

The Methods describe paired Student t tests on pre/post differences, with P<.05, but do not fully specify how each Table 2 comparison was constructed. The narrative reports a 15% MVC increase and near doubling of fatigue resistance during creatine, with no changes during placebo; placebo stiffness increased by about 25%, while creatine-period stiffness did not change. **Credit the table’s reported direct functional comparisons, while retaining the incompletely specified analysis.** Do not reinterpret the narrative percentages as placebo-adjusted effect sizes or create a treatment interaction from differing within-period P values.

Figure 1 shows the two sequences normalized to their first measurement. Results and Discussion explicitly state that the MVC and fatigue effects remained after washout and returned toward initial values only after the subsequent placebo period. That persistence may be biologically interesting, but it also undermines a clean crossover contrast: a later placebo period can follow residual treatment effects. The paper gives no formal carryover test, period/sequence adjustment or separately analyzed first-period treatment contrast. Progressive disease and growth further complicate pooling periods. No new analysis or numerical extraction from the plots was performed here.

The paper does not designate a primary endpoint, provide an original sample-size plan, or report multiplicity correction across numerous outcomes and subgroups. The reported functional positives are therefore nominal and not a confirmed prespecified primary hierarchy. Absence of a clear hierarchy does not erase them.

## Biomarkers, bone findings and nulls

The PCr/β-ATP row reports P=.04 in the table’s placebo-versus-creatine column. The Results and Discussion instead describe an approximately 8% creatine-period rise that did not reach significance on the pre/post test. These can concern different comparisons, but the reporting does not resolve their exact relationship. Preserve both rather than silently selecting a positive or null mechanism conclusion. The pre-creatine and post-washout ratios were 3.71 ± 0.34 and 3.68 ± 0.31 (NS). Neither result proves that altered energy metabolism mediated the functional effect.

Whole-cohort Table 2 comparisons are NS for body mass, lumbar-spine and whole-body bone density, urine NTx/creatinine, urinary creatinine, albumin excretion, plasma creatinine, AST, ALT, γ-glutamyltransferase and creatine kinase. Plasma creatine and daily urinary creatine increased (both P<.001 in the table). The Discussion also states that lean body mass did not increase, but the referenced table lists **body mass**, not a separate lean-mass row. Do not substitute body mass for measured lean tissue or muscle renewal.

In the five boys who remained ambulatory throughout the study, the Results and Figure 2 report within-period bone findings:

| Outcome | Placebo, pre → post | Creatine, pre → post |
|---|---|---|
| Lumbar-spine density, g/cm² | 0.604 ± 0.0426 → 0.604 ± 0.0442; NS | 0.601 ± 0.0476 → 0.624 ± 0.0498; P<.05 |
| Whole-body density, g/cm² | 0.776 ± 0.0215 → 0.781 ± 0.0189; NS | 0.776 ± 0.0190 → 0.792 ± 0.0227; P<.05 |
| Urinary NTx/creatinine, mmol BCE/mmol creatinine as printed | 1144 ± 204 → 1325 ± 264; NS | 1365 ± 245 → 599 ± 154; P<.001 |

The abstract summarizes bone-density gain as about 3% and NTx excretion as falling to about one third; the exact main-text ratio values above remain separately retained without reconciliation. The authors report no effect in wheelchair-dependent boys. The five-person findings do not establish an ambulatory-status interaction, a placebo-adjusted bone effect or independent replication. Bone and NTx changes are not additional functional endpoints. The proposed common growth-signaling explanation is a hypothesis; neither tissue renewal nor dystrophin/BUBR1 repair was demonstrated.

## Harms, planning and disclosures

The authors report no cramps, diarrhea or other side effects, and no adverse hepatic or renal effect. The reported creatinine, albumin and enzyme comparisons were NS. This is limited observed tolerability in a selected small cohort; the report supplies no graded adverse-event table, systematic collection schedule or exposure-adjusted harm analysis. It does not establish generic or long-term safety, and nonsignificant laboratory differences do not prove equivalence.

The university ethics committee approved the study, and consent from the boys and both parents is reported. No registry identifier, prospectively dated protocol, endpoint hierarchy or separate supplement was identified in the main or bounded searches. Current ClinicalTrials.gov exact PMID/DOI and exact-title/investigator searches returned no studies. This does not prove that the trial was never registered elsewhere. Ethics approval is not a recovered prespecified analysis plan.

Flamma provided creatine. Grants came from the Association Française contre les Myopathies, Fonds National de la Recherche Scientifique and Fondation Van-Goethem-Brichant. Magali Louis was funded by the Université Catholique de Louvain’s Fonds du Patrimoine pour la recherche médicale. No separate conflict-of-interest declaration was identified; absence of such a section is not proof of no conflicts.
