# Mebendazole / YTHDC1 / MASLD — primary qualification

**Retain as an actual-agent protein-availability and other-tissue protection lead, with limited mechanistic qualification.** Mebendazole raises YTHDC1 protein abundance and improves liver pathology and lipid/injury measures in high-fat-diet mice. The evidence exceeds an isolated anti-inflammatory association. It does **not** establish that the drug reverses YTHDC1 lactylation or ubiquitination, prolongs its cellular half-life, restores mutant protein function, or improves MVA muscle function.

Source: Zhang et al., *Advanced Science*, DOI [10.1002/advs.202510192](https://doi.org/10.1002/advs.202510192), [PMID 41298230 / PMC13067837](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13067837/). First published November 26, 2025; assigned to April 2026 issue. Lawful open-access primary XML, all main figure files, and the original supplementary DOCX were obtained from Europe PMC. No access bypass or citation cascade.

## What was actually administered and measured

Figure 7 compares normal-diet control, HFD control, HFD plus mebendazole 30 or 60 mg/kg, and HFD plus atorvastatin 20 mg/kg. The methods identify mebendazole as MedChemExpress HY-17595, given daily by oral gavage; controls receive saline. These are reported research exposures, not recommendations. Figure 7B shows four weeks of diet before treatment, followed by eight weeks of treatment, with harvest at week 12. The methods inconsistently describe control/atorvastatin diet duration as eight weeks and mebendazole diet duration as twelve; the Results wording also admits a misleading sequential interpretation. The figure supports a twelve-week total schedule, but exact group timing needs author clarification. Drug formulation, cell mebendazole concentration and target-engagement incubation details are not reported in the accessible methods/supplement.

Both mebendazole groups show less Oil Red O staining and improved representative H&E appearances, lower body/liver weights, serum/liver cholesterol and triglycerides, serum LDL-C, and serum/liver ALT/AST versus HFD controls. Figure 7 reports n=4 for histology and staining quantification and n=12 for weights/biochemistry. These are animal/tissue measurements; histological sampling independence is not specified. The assays measure steatosis, pathology, lipid handling and injury markers. They do not measure liver synthetic/clearance capacity, survival, physical performance or muscle force. Complete normalization and superiority to atorvastatin are not established by the displayed comparisons.

## Protein mechanism: supported and missing

Supplement S10A–B shows greater YTHDC1 resistance to heating and pronase in mebendazole-treated HepG2 material (n=3), consistent with target engagement. Docking predicts an interaction; neither assay supplies a purified-protein affinity constant or establishes target exclusivity. S10C shows increased liver YTHDC1 with high-dose mebendazole (n=6; P=.007 versus HFD), accompanied by reduced PTPN22 and NLRP3 (S10D–E). No direct drug experiment measures restored YTHDC1 RNA-reader activity, PTPN22 mRNA turnover, YTHDC1 half-life, or reduced YTHDC1 lactylation/ubiquitination.

The broader paper supports a biologically coherent pathway using **separate interventions**: Ythdc1 knockout/overexpression, AARS1 or LDHA silencing, K565A YTHDC1 mutation, the monocarboxylate-transporter inhibitor CHC, and proteasome inhibition with MG132. Figure 3 links lactylation to ubiquitination/protein loss; MG132 preserves YTHDC1 whereas chloroquine does not show the same restoration. These observations must not be reassigned to mebendazole. The cycloheximide chase concerns **PTPN22 protein stability under genetic YTHDC1 manipulation**, not mebendazole-induced YTHDC1 stabilization.

**Material control limitation:** S10F–L compares HFD+high-dose mebendazole with HFD+high-dose mebendazole+hepatocyte Ythdc1 knockout. It does not present the genotype-by-drug factorial or a concurrent knockout+vehicle group. Knockout alone worsens MASLD elsewhere (Figure 2). Thus worse outcomes in treated knockout mice support involvement of YTHDC1 but do not establish that mebendazole's within-genotype benefit is abolished. The authors' dependency wording is stronger than this comparison warrants. S10 reports n=4 histology and n=6 biochemical measurements.

## Quality, harms and nomination consequence

This is one primary preclinical report with convergent molecular/genetic observations and an active drug comparator. The accessible report does not describe randomization, blinding, sex, prospective sample-size calculation or exclusions. Its statistics use t-tests or one-way ANOVA with Dunnett correction, reporting mean±SEM and at least three independent experiments; the identity of every replicate is not resolved. It explicitly states that research data are not shared. No normal-diet mebendazole arm, food-intake/pair-feeding comparison, systematic toxicity assessment, mortality/attrition accounting, blood counts or chromosome-segregation safety assessment is reported. Weight reduction therefore does not by itself resolve caloric-intake or tolerability explanations. Human liver comparisons use noncancerous tissue adjacent to cancer lesions as controls and are not drug-treatment evidence. A screening library's regulatory label does not establish eligibility or safety of this prolonged regimen.

For the MVA objective, credit this as a plausible **pharmacological increase in available wild-type protein linked to liver protection**. It is stronger than a biomarker-only nomination, yet usable molecular function and the proposed stabilization route remain incompletely established. It neither repairs BUB1B nor tests mitotic fidelity or MVA tissue function. Relative to the current niacin restoration and apitegromab compensation nominees, it merits retention as a mechanistic competitor but supplies no direct MVA efficacy and no muscle-performance evidence that would justify displacement. The limitation is the missing mechanistic/functional bridge, not the fact that the positive tissue is liver.
