# Beta-alanine has a controlled fatigue-threshold signal, with strength and training limits

23 September 2026. **Retain beta-alanine as a functional research comparator.** The positive randomized trial isolates its addition to a nutritional drink and supports an EMG-estimated fatigue-threshold workload benefit. It does not demonstrate broad strength improvement, independent living, BubR1 restoration or benefit in congenital muscle disease. The separate training trial reports no beta-alanine advantage, but its full main remains inaccessible in this pass.

## Positive trial: an additive contrast on the same nutritional background

[McCormack et al., PMID23832078](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.exger.2013.06.003) randomized 60 older volunteers (mean 70.7±6.2 years) to a common Ensure High Protein drink, or that same-batch drink with non-sustained-release CarnoSyn beta-alanine powder. Each serving contained 230 kcal, 12 g protein, 31 g carbohydrate and 6 g fat. Two daily servings supplied either **1.6 or 2.4 g/day beta-alanine for 12 weeks**. These are studied exposures, not dosing advice. Matching the drink isolates the added ingredient within this nutritional context; it does not establish the same effect without the drink or with another formulation.

Only 44 participants completed follow-up: control 16/20, lower-dose 15/19 and higher-dose 13/21. The analysis is of completers; no ITT or missing-outcome imputation was identified. Adherence by returned bottles/caps was 95%, 93% and 90%, respectively. Most completers were already physically active, and no sarcopenia criterion was established. Normal exercise routines were continued rather than assigned. Baseline group characteristics, including sex composition, were uneven after attrition. Randomization and double blinding are stated, but generation, concealment and blinding success are not documented in the main.

The paper reports the following distinct findings; values below are transcribed from the original PDF's readable text, with native pixels unverified:

| Endpoint | Controlled finding | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Physical working capacity at fatigue threshold (PWCFT) | Treatment×time **P=.005**, partial η²=.23. Abstract reports both fortified-arm changes greater than control, **P<.05**. | EMG-estimated workload during submaximal cycling; not measured maximal force, walking distance or sustained task duration. No pairwise effect CI supplied. |
| Absolute maximum/average grip | No significant treatment×time effect. | No numerical treatment estimate or CI supplied; null is not equivalence. |
| Grip divided by arm lean tissue mass | Interaction **P=.039**; baseline-adjusted higher-dose increase greater than both other arms, **P<.05**, per Figure 1 caption. | A ratio endpoint. Higher-dose within-arm P=.06; it must not be recast as increased absolute grip strength. Exact adjusted contrasts and native graph values remain unverified. |
| 30-second chair stands | Interaction **P=.024**; adjusted higher-dose versus lower-dose **P=.021**. | Higher-dose superiority over drink-only control is not reported. Significant within-arm improvement also occurred in control. |
| Body composition | No significant bodyweight, lean-tissue or fat-mass change. | No general hypertrophy claim. |

PWCFT means were 50.8→58.8 W in the higher-dose arm, 47.7→57.3 W in the lower-dose arm and 59.4→55.9 W in control. Table 5 prints 13.6%, 17.8% and −6.3%; these are retained as **source-reported percentages**, not asserted ordinary percentage changes calculated from baseline group means. Their calculation basis is unspecified and some do not match that simple calculation. Higher versus lower dose was not significantly different (P=.547), so there is no established dose gradient.

PWCFT uses the workload associated with a significant rise in vastus-lateralis EMG amplitude across two-minute stages, with an alternate regression estimate when the threshold is not reached. The number needing that alternate estimator is unreported. This is a relevant exercise response, but converting it into reduced falls or restored daily independence would require another endpoint. No familiarization was provided. Control repeat chair-stand performance increased significantly during the reliability check (P=.026), which further cautions against interpreting within-arm gains as supplement effects.

The statistics used treatment×time ANOVA, baseline-adjusted ANCOVA and Tukey follow-up comparisons, with paired tests for within-group change. No study-wide correction across endpoint families, registered endpoint hierarchy or protocol was established. The planned minimum was 12/arm for 80% power, derived from an earlier study; effect-size assumptions were not supplied. Neither CIs nor an inferential reconstruction from arm summaries is provided here.

## Harms and the independent training context

The higher-dose group had **six reported paresthesia cases, including two withdrawals**; the lower-dose group had one continuing case. The paper reports the PWCFT significance persisted after excluding symptomatic participants, without complete sensitivity estimates. Such exclusion does not prove successful masking. CBC/chemistry was measured in completers; several nominal within-arm changes are shown in Table 4. The authors' conclusion that no measure newly moved outside a reference range does not establish comprehensive, long-term or pediatric safety. The study was funded by Abbott Nutrition; JR Stout disclosed compensated presentations for Abbott.

[Bailey et al., PMID29336621](https://doi.org/10.1080/19390211.2017.1406422) reports a 12-week randomized double-blind study in 27 adults aged 60–82, using 3.2 g/day beta-alanine or placebo with or without resistance training. The publisher acknowledgment identifies sustained-release tablets and matched placebo supplied by Natural Alternatives International. The abstract's leg-press 1RM interaction (P=.010) was attributable to training improvements, **with no significant beta-alanine-specific between-group difference**. No beta-alanine advantage was reported for the 20-repetition chest/leg-press outcomes. Positive within-training power/fatigue comparisons are not drug efficacy.

This is meaningful counterevidence for that training protocol, not proof that every beta-alanine exposure is ineffective. Per-arm denominators, missingness, exact ADL tests, adherence, harms, effect CIs and full analysis details remain unknown because only the original abstract and publisher disclosures were accessible. Different authors/institutions, dose and design distinguish it from 2013; registry-based cohort independence was not verified.

## Mechanism, identity and competitive decision

The 2013 study explicitly did **not measure muscle carnosine**. Buffering and calcium-sensitivity mechanisms are proposed from other sources, not demonstrated mediation. No BUB1B/BubR1, checkpoint or chromosome-fidelity endpoint is reported in its complete scientific text; the 2018 abstract likewise supplies no such evidence. Neither beta-alanine nor carnosine may inherit the other's trial outcomes.

The controlled fatigue-threshold and adjusted ratio signals warrant retention. They are stronger treatment-control evidence than an uncontrolled before–after observation, while remaining less direct evidence of improved force or daily motor function. Attrition, formulation, endpoint and model limits explain the reserve status without selectively requiring MVA proof that current nominees also lack. No cross-trial MVA superiority or automatic nominee change follows.

NIH DSLD group 1061 identifies beta-alanine but also groups salts/esters. The integrated entry therefore leaves automatic matching empty and preserves exact source aliases; generic alanine, carnosine, benzoyl/amidino derivatives, mixtures and arbitrary retail products must not inherit efficacy. Inventory membership is not approval status.
