# Kidney protection without BUBR1-restoration evidence

[Nishizono et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5619973/) provides
actual-drug renal-function evidence for rapamycin and enalapril in a transgenic
rat with impaired podocyte adaptation to growth. This is neither BUB1B
deficiency nor a demonstrated renal phenotype in the current case.

| Intervention | Positive result retained | Load-bearing distinction |
|---|---|---|
| Rapamycin, alternate-day exposure | Preserved weight-normalized creatinine clearance and reduced proteinuria/adhesions. | Weekly exposure was not significantly different from untreated controls on those endpoints. Overall growth slowed with alternate-day treatment. |
| Enalapril | Preserved clearance and reduced proteinuria/adhesions without a significant body-weight difference. | Blood pressure also fell; an isolated growth mechanism and general developmental safety are not established. |
| Delayed drug initiation | Partial lesion/proteinuria protection after injury had begun. | Delayed-arm clearance was not shown; benefit cannot be assigned uniformly to every endpoint or schedule. |

Three-week tissue cohorts had 5–7 animals/group; long-term arm denominators
were not identified. Doses, schedules, figure comparisons and source limits are
recorded in the [structured assessment](renal-growth-qualification.json). They describe experiments, not a patient
regimen. Histologic scoring was blinded; general allocation concealment and
experimental blinding were not stated.

Bulk glomerular Bub1b RNA fell with all three protective interventions. The
supplement reports log2 changes of −0.932 for calorie reduction, −0.808 for
rapamycin and −0.481 for enalapril. These population-level measurements do not
establish protein loss within a cell, a direct drug effect on BUBR1 stability,
or a causal contribution to benefit. Reduced cycling and altered cell
composition remain alternative explanations.

## Consequence for the comparison

Credit the renal functional evidence. It prevents dismissing these agents on
the basis that they have only marker effects. Keep the research questions
separate: preventing a kidney from outgrowing its podocyte coverage differs
from restoring usable BUBR1 or increasing muscle output. An independently
established matching renal lesion would be needed to make enalapril a case
specific reserve; nephrocalcinosis alone does not supply that mechanism.

The rapamycin result cannot be assigned to everolimus or an arbitrary mTOR
exposure. A renal benefit also cannot establish favorable whole-organism tradeoffs
when growth is suppressed. Neither issue proves a drug will harm this patient;
both constrain what this experiment supports. Current nominees remain subject
to the same missing MVA-efficacy evidence.

Calorie restriction is retained as context, not another drug. The supplement's
muscle-loss interpretation relies on urinary creatinine excretion, not direct
strength or muscle-mass measurement. Dietary mixtures and sucrose comparisons
do not identify a transferable single-compound treatment.

Delayed 20% calorie reduction lowered kidney weight, but did not significantly
reduce proteinuria or adhesions in Figure 5. Its effects cannot be described
as either uniform protection or a null on every endpoint.

## Reading boundary

The complete substantive main text, six main tables and all seven figure
legends were read; Figures 3–5 were inspected visually. The 17-page supplement
was read selectively: complete Table 1 and legend, Table 2 heading/Bub1b row/
legend, and Table 3 heading/Bub1b row/legend, with the relevant pages rendered.
The whole 538-gene table and linked editorial were not appraised. Original
papers, figure images and reading material remain outside the repository.

A second reader checked the main Methods/Results, Figures 3–5 and selected
supplement pages. This narrowed the delayed calorie-restriction interpretation;
it was not an independent review of every source.
