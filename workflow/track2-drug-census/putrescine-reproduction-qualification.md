# Putrescine: reproductive outcomes in older mice

Reviewed 23 September 2026. Integrated as one exact-salt assessment, with no new unique abstract credit or experimental replication.

**Putrescine dihydrochloride merits a nonmalignant reproductive-function reserve.** A 2015 mouse study reports reduced embryo resorption in two strains and increased blastocyst cell counts after a short drinking-water intervention. Its larger aggregate live-pup count is an encouraging observed outcome, but neither the proportion of females producing litters nor mean litter size reached significance. A separate 2013 paper reports lower egg aneuploidy after combined oral and in-vitro supplementation; its full methods and data remain inaccessible in this review. Neither source currently establishes BUB1B/BubR1 restoration or benefit in MVA. These findings strengthen a common-molecule comparison set without displacing the provisional niacin/apitegromab hypotheses on evidence of MVA benefit; those hypotheses also remain unproven.

## What was administered in 2015

The Methods specify **putrescine dihydrochloride, BioReagent grade, Sigma P-5780**, at **1% w/v (reported 62 mM)** in drinking water. This is a defined salt preparation; no free-base, dietary-food, generic polyamine, or consumer-product equivalence is inferred. Individual water intake and achieved oral mg/kg exposure were not reported; the authors only describe no noticeable consumption difference.

CF1 females were 9–10-month-old retired breeders, received treated or control water for four days before natural mating, and stopped treatment upon a vaginal plug or after four mating nights. They were examined at 12.5 days post coitum (dpc); young controls were three months old. C57BL/6 females received male bedding and two days of premating treatment, stopped upon a plug or after two mating nights, and were studied at 10 months for resorption (9.5 dpc) or 10–11 months for births (daily checks at 16.5–23.5 dpc). Males also drank treated water during mating and were rotated between groups in subsequent experiments. That design reduces one male-allocation concern but does not isolate an oocyte-mediated effect.

A separate young-CF1 pharmacokinetic experiment used **100 mg/kg subcutaneous putrescine**, three mice per time point. It showed transient serum/ovarian exposure and rapid decline. This bolus is neither the reproductive efficacy regimen nor a measurement of oral bioavailability. The ovarian-content cohorts also differ in age from the reproductive cohorts: Figure 2B uses seven-month CF1 mice, and Figure 2C uses twelve-month C57BL/6 mice. Ovarian putrescine increased with supplementation; spermidine/spermine were measured metabolites, not separately tested beneficial interventions. DFMO inhibition and cited ODC genetics supply mechanistic context, not interchangeable treatment evidence.

Resorption statistics use implantation sites, while treatment is assigned to mice and multiple embryos share each mother. The reported Fisher analyses do not model maternal clustering. Random allocation, blinded classification, and cohort/batch replication are not specified in the read Methods. Two strains are useful within-study extension, not independent laboratory replication. Resorption classifications rely on uterine/placental dissection; the studies do not show direct chromosome fidelity in these same reproductive cohorts.

The 2015 study used natural mating, not IVF or embryo transfer. Its Discussion explicitly identifies IVF/transfer as needed to rule out male or uterine contributions. The proposal that improved oocyte quality mediates the result is plausible but not isolated experimentally here. No Bub1b RNA, BubR1 protein, checkpoint function, or MVA model was reported in the read scientific text.

## Aneuploidy and safety boundaries

The 2013 complete abstract reports a combined drinking-water plus in-vitro maturation intervention in eight-month-old mice, reducing egg aneuploidy **12.7% to 5.3%**. It does not expose the supplementation dose, salt, arm structure, sample denominators, statistical test, or whether chromosome assessment covers the whole karyotype versus selected FISH targets. The publisher's supplementary listing mentions euploid/hypoploid/hyperploid images and karyotype tables, but those labels cannot resolve the missing assay details. The 2015 citation to this finding is not a substitute for reading the 2013 source. Oral and IVM contributions must remain unresolved.

Table II in 2015 describes small breeding observations: treated young-mouse groups of three to five, and offspring breeding groups of two or three, with normal pups reported. The main text says both male and female offspring grew and reproduced normally, but the reader extraction corrupts Table II sex symbols; sex-specific row assignment is not independently resolved. These observations do not establish long-term, multigenerational, comprehensive developmental, or human safety. Rapid subcutaneous clearance and genetic ODC-overexpression references do not fill those gaps. The authors' suggested remedy for older women exceeds the evidence reviewed.

**Recommended disposition:** retain the measured dihydrochloride reproductive benefit as an experimental functional reserve, with a separate access-limited aneuploidy lead. The integrated appraisal preserves the salt and regimen rather than automatically inherit generic putrescine catalog identity. The highest-value unfinished work is lawful retrieval of the 2013 main/supplement and the 2015 native figures/supplementary mother-level tables.
