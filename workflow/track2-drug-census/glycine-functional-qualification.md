# Glycine alone: two human trials, with a material ICU access limit

Glycine alone has a controlled signal for bioimpedance-derived fat-free mass in a small haemodialysis crossover trial. That trial did not report a between-supplement benefit in strength, activity or quality of life. A later ICU trial clearly demonstrates enteral availability and increased muscle glycine; its reported favourable functional and clinical directions deserve retention, but the numerical functional tables and complete main could not be inspected through the lawful routes checked. Neither source establishes a BUBR1 response or efficacy in MVA. This integrated qualification supports a separate glycine appraisal; it does not change the research nominations or establish a treatment recommendation.

The analysis concerns glycine itself. Existing GlyNAC evidence belongs to the combination of glycine and N-acetylcysteine and is not inherited by either ingredient. The read-only repository at `609edd2b7864ec939f9a620aa00bc8c7f08767f4` contained 247 decisions and a combination-specific `new-glynac` entry; no standalone glycine decision was found. The optional 2019 myotube paper was not needed to interpret these human sources and was not opened or credited.

## Genton 2021: a mass signal with a nonpositive controlled functional comparison

The trial enrolled adults on maintenance haemodialysis with features of protein-energy wasting, including low intake and low screening albumin. Of 37 randomized individuals, one withdrew consent before baseline and the treatment was reassigned; 36 started and contributed adverse-event surveillance. Twenty-seven compliant completers were analysed: 15 began BCAA and 12 began glycine. This is a per-protocol complete-crossover population, not an intention-to-treat analysis of all randomized patients. The main does not fully enumerate the reasons for the remaining losses. Mean age among completers was 61.2 years; 11 were women. These are dialysis patients, not an MVA, BUB1B-variant or general healthy population.

Participants received two four-month supplement periods separated by a one-month washout (observed 31 ± 4 days). Glycine was 7 g per pack twice daily, 30 minutes before breakfast and dinner: 14 g/day orally. The comparator was a BCAA mixture, per pack 3.62 g leucine, 1.94 g valine and 1.45 g isoleucine, also twice daily. Bichsel pharmacy prepared matched granules; Ivers-Lee packaged them under current good manufacturing practice. These are study preparations, not evidence that arbitrary retail products are equivalent. Glycine was intended as the comparison preparation, but both periods supplied biologically active amino acids; there was no unsupplemented or inert-placebo period. Empty/full pack counting defined compliance above 80% in each period. Randomization was individual sequence assignment, stratified by centre with blocks of two and four; a later added clinic used remaining packs from existing site allocations.

The paper explicitly calls this a **post hoc analysis** of a trial whose primary outcome was gut microbiota. The current registry lists microbiota composition and function as primary; DXA/BIA composition, handgrip, seven-day pedometry, intake and SF-36 quality of life are among many secondary outcomes. Registration was first submitted August 23, 2016 and first posted November 11, 2016; study start is August 2016, and the main specifies August 1. Thus the inspected dates do not establish prospective public registration before study start. Historical registry versions and a separate protocol/SAP were not read. Current endpoint listing should not be promoted to proof of the original analysis plan.

The models included supplement, month, period, age and sex as fixed effects and subject random intercepts, using repeated measurements within participants rather than treating the two periods as independent parallel groups. Some outcomes were transformed for normality. The paper used Benjamini–Hochberg correction, with Table 4 reporting a supplement threshold below .005 for that outcome family. It examined supplement-by-month and period-by-month interactions; period-by-month was not significant. It did not test carryover, citing its chosen crossover guidance. A washout and a nonsignificant period interaction do not independently prove absence of residual carryover. The secondary lean-mass sample-size calculation used a large assumed change derived from a different comparison, and the study was not established as adequately powered for every functional outcome.

BIA estimates total fat-free components and is sensitive to hydration; it does not directly isolate new muscle protein or force. Assessments occurred shortly after dialysis, and the authors compared interdialytic weight gain and supplemental dialysis requirements. Their observations reduce a simple fluid-overload explanation but do not prove that the entire BIA effect is contractile muscle. The null DXA result remains material. Plasma glycine did not increase at the sampled visits; timing after oral exposure is the authors' possible explanation, not a measured mechanism. No muscle biopsy, BUBR1 assay, chromosome-fidelity assay or variant rescue was reported in the scientific main read.

Safety was assessed in 36 exposed participants. No deaths occurred. The authors report 11 patients hospitalized with serious events and judge them unrelated to supplementation. Table 1 is labelled **episodes**, not independent-patient risk: nausea/vomiting is 6 during glycine, 1 during BCAA and 3 during washout; the Discussion describes six glycine patients, three also symptomatic during washout. Attribution remains uncertain. Additional dialysis for fluid overload occurred in four patients, with multiple sessions concentrated in one patient. The main reports no statistically significant adverse-event difference; a small study is not evidence of equivalence or absence of risk.

Funding included the Swiss National Science Foundation, Alfred and Alice Lachmann Foundation and Fresenius Kabi. The paper states funders had no role in design/conduct, analysis/interpretation or manuscript approval. Genton and Teta disclosed multiple industry relationships; Cani disclosed a biotech cofounder role and patents. Those disclosures are retained without treating them as proof of invalidity.

## ICU 2025: target exposure is verified; functional interpretation remains incomplete

The abstract describes a blinded, randomized, three-group study in mechanically ventilated adults receiving usual ICU care: placebo **n=10**, glycine **0.2 g/kg/day n=9**, or **0.4 g/kg/day n=10**, for up to seven days. The registry describes nasogastric administration in sterile water, in two daily infusions, ending at extubation or seven days; all groups received standard enteral nutrition. The initial absorption test used approximately 0.0667 or 0.1333 g/kg glycine with 100 mL standard feed; controls received feed without added glycine. The article's abstract gives actual daily median (IQR) exposure of **16.2 (4.6–16.4) g** and **29.6 (24.8–32.4) g** in the low and high groups. This short enteral critical-care regimen is not interchangeable with Genton's four-month oral regimen. Glycine supplier, purity and batch were not verified from the accessible excerpts.

The primary endpoint was the plasma glycine concentration AUC over 240 minutes. The abstract reports a high-dose versus placebo mean difference of **759.40 μmol/L·min**, 95% CI **219.27 to 1299.54**, **P=.008**. This is an exposure/absorption result, not a strength outcome. The article excerpts report muscle glycine concentration differences versus placebo of **+0.10 nmol/mg wet muscle** for low dose (95% CI **0.02 to 0.18**, P=.017) and **+0.16** for high dose (95% CI **0.08 to 0.23**, P=.001). The Figure 3 caption states metabolomics n=25, fibre histology n=23 and pressure-ultrasound n=27. The text states 53 biopsies in 28 participants, with 25 paired; biopsy consent was absent for one participant. Those outcome denominators must not be replaced by all 29 randomized.

Pressure-ultrasound quadriceps thickness point estimates favoured less loss but were not statistically conclusive: high dose versus placebo **+0.11 cm**, 95% CI **−0.10 to 0.31**, **P=.299**; low dose **+0.06 cm**, 95% CI **−0.14 to 0.26**, **P=.555**. The abstract/excerpts report no myofibre diameter/area benefit. A directionally favourable thickness estimate is not established prevention of wasting. Four participants lacked usable no-pressure ultrasound; biopsy quality and fibre orientation also limited histological analysis.

The publisher-indexed Results explicitly describe higher FIM motor scores seven days after ICU discharge in the glycine groups, and shorter ICU/hospital admissions and higher EQ-5D-5L visual-analogue scores. They describe MRC scores below the ICU-weakness threshold in placebo/low dose but not high dose among assessable patients. **These potentially useful signals are retained, not converted into nulls.** However, Table S4 and main Table 4 could not be read. Exact functional denominators, values, treatment CIs/P values, handling of death or inability to participate, missingness, and adjustment cannot be verified. A threshold crossed by one group's score is not itself a randomized treatment comparison. The accessible record does not justify a precise controlled strength/function effect, an assumed significant FIM benefit, or a claim that all randomized patients were functionally assessed.

The current ANZCTR display lists absorption as primary and FIM, dynamometer grip, PFIT-s, morphology, quality of life and clinical outcomes as secondary. It describes concealed computerized randomization and masking of recipients, treating personnel, assessors and analysts. Planned AUC regression includes age and APACHE II; morphology/thickness analyses adjust baseline, and the registry uses nominal **P<.05 for all tests** without a stated family-wise or false-discovery correction. The accessible article excerpt describes baseline-adjusted linear regression and refers to additional age/illness-severity adjustment. Full statistical reporting and any separate SAP remain unreviewed.

Registration on March 21, 2018 precedes the registry's actual first enrolment of May 21, 2018; final enrolment is July 27, 2022 and final sample 29 of target 30. Current registry displays were read, not archived original versions. Two same-URL web renderings disagreed on last-update display (October 18 versus June 25, 2025) while both showed results provided October 18, 2025; this rendering difference is not evidence of a changed scientific endpoint. No supporting document was attached in the inspected record. Registry funding names Royal Melbourne Hospital and University of Melbourne; Crossref additionally identifies the Australian and New Zealand Intensive Care Foundation and NHMRC. The full article's funding-role/conflict and adverse-event sections were not accessed. Similar peak urea/creatinine in an excerpt cannot replace a complete safety assessment.

No BUBR1 response, MVA rescue or chromosome-fidelity outcome is reported in the abstract, excerpts or registry read. This is a reading-scope statement, not a claim to have searched every assay in an inaccessible main or supplement, and not a measured BUBR1 null.

## Availability and comparative use

The exact [DailyMed glycine label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=c10a4657-cf64-4a71-a247-363dd58993bc) inspected describes **1.5% glycine irrigation**, NDA018315, for transurethral urologic procedures. That formulation/route approval does not establish oral supplement approval, muscle efficacy or equivalence to either study preparation. Its prescribing text is revised November 2022; the page's current archive entry is version 9, June 22, 2026. Catalogue presence or availability must remain separate from evidence of efficacy. No dosing advice is inferred from trial exposures.

For comparison with the existing niacin appraisal, the same distinction between controlled treatment effects and uncontrolled/within-arm functional changes should apply. The dialysis study's randomized BIA signal deserves credit despite lacking an MVA cohort; its within-period handgrip P value deserves the same causal caution as uncontrolled strength evidence elsewhere. The ICU source supplies real exposure and tissue data and reported functional directions, with an unresolved numerical-source gap. Absence of direct MVA evidence is shared uncertainty, not a selectively stricter eligibility hurdle. These trials do not resolve glycine-versus-niacin MVA efficacy, establish a cross-trial functional ranking, or support inheriting GlyNAC/NAC results.

The bounded review is complete at this access depth. The decisive remaining source needs are Genton's authentic supplement (especially functional Table S4) and the ICU complete main, Table 4 and supplement Table S4, with their exact outcome denominators and contrasts. They are access gaps, not requests for new compound discovery. The source reviewer changed no repository file, decision object or existing frozen source package.
