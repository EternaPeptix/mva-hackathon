# Cherrypharm cherry–apple blend: acute strength preservation, limited transfer

**Retain the exact tested Cherrypharm blend as a functional research reserve.** A small randomized crossover study reports less loss of measured isometric strength after damaging exercise. A later parallel study found no detectable recovery benefit with a different tart-cherry concentrate. Both results matter: they concern different preparations and do not establish a generic cherry effect, chronic weakness treatment, BUBR1 restoration or MVA efficacy.

This appraisal concerns the 2006 Cherrypharm **Montmorency cherry–apple blend**. The 2019 CherryActive concentrate and its separate pomegranate arm are identified counterevidence, not interchangeable aliases or additional positive nominations. No pooled effect or head-to-head potency ranking is calculated.

## The controlled functional positive

[Connolly et al., 2006](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsm.2005.025429) studied healthy young men without recent upper-body strength training. Sixteen started and 14 completed both crossover periods. Each period paired a drink regimen with 40 maximal eccentric elbow-flexion contractions; the opposite arm was challenged in the second period. A transducer measured voluntary isometric force at three angles. The published outcomes are percentages of each period's baseline, not an absolute Newton treatment contrast.

| Result | Cherrypharm blend | Control | Actual comparison |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Average strength loss over four follow-up days | 4% (SD 15) | 22% (SD 12) | Treatment×time and reported average comparison P<.0001 |
| Strength loss at 24 hours | 12% | 30% | Native Figure 1: P<.01 |
| Strength at 96 hours relative to baseline | 6% above | 12% below | Native Figure 1: P<.001 |
| Average pain, 0–10 scale | 2.4 (SD 0.7) | 3.2 (SD 1.1) | P=.051; time-course interaction P=.017 |

The arithmetic difference in average strength loss is **18 percentage points**; a paired effect confidence interval was not supplied. All four daily strength comparisons have Bonferroni correction, which deserves credit. Motion and tenderness outcomes were not statistically significant. The figures show SE, while the narrative reports SD. These are repeated measurements from 14 people, not independent cohorts at each time or angle.

Both withdrawals were blend-first participants who left school, leaving five blend-first and nine control-first completers. The five blend-first participants still had less strength loss with the blend than in their later control period (3% versus 24%, interaction P=.001). This small Discussion reanalysis argues against a simple universal second-period advantage; it does not replace a full carryover/period analysis or missing-sequence sensitivity.

The second protocol began two weeks after the initial baseline, six days after the first period ended. It was not a two-week treatment-free washout. Randomization is reported, but sequence generation, concealment and masking implementation are not supplied. The opposite-arm design avoids repeating injury in the same muscle; it does not prove the absence of systemic, cross-limb, learning or treatment carryover. The sample-size target was 16, anchored to strength loss. A prospective single-primary hierarchy and correction across all endpoint families were not verified.

Baseline force was measured **before drinks were supplied**, with no immediate pre-exercise re-baseline reported. The result therefore preserves useful post-challenge function but cannot isolate a prechallenge strength change from reduced damage or faster recovery. Structural muscle protection and antioxidant/anti-inflammatory mediation were not demonstrated; CK and myoglobin were not measured. This was supplementation before and after a challenge, not post-injury-only treatment or a chronic-disease trial.

## Preparation identity and the later counterevidence

The original blend used juice freshly prepared from frozen Montmorency cherries plus commercial apple juice in an undisclosed proprietary ratio. It was pasteurized and bottled; it was not a concentrate or isolated anthocyanin. The source regimen was two 12-fl-oz bottles daily for eight days, with the challenge on the fourth supplementation day. This is a study description, not a dosing recommendation.

Its control was black-cherry Kool-Aid/water with sugar added to match 13 Brix soluble solids, followed by the same processing. The listed mix included ascorbic acid in an unreported amount. Do not call the control inert, plain sugar water or analytically calorie-matched. No apple-only control separates the ingredients' contributions.

[Lamb et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu11071593) instead tested CherryActive concentrate diluted with water and maltodextrin, alongside POM Wonderful pomegranate juice and an energy-matched blackcurrant sports-drink control. The tested cherry cultivar and lot were not explicitly identified. The Methods specify two routine 250-mL servings per day for nine days, plus an extra serving after the day-five challenge—not four routine daily servings. Participants received a standardized post-exercise meal.

The 2019 assay's ± dispersion is **not identified as SD or SEM**. These are assay-equivalent quantities, not doses of a proven active molecule or measured tissue exposure. Different composition could contribute to different outcomes, but neither study demonstrates that explanation. The higher-phenolic pomegranate arm also lacked detectable recovery benefit in the later trial.

The later trial randomized 36 non-resistance-trained men, 12 per arm; all completed follow-up. Native strength figures nevertheless use **11 per arm** after missing visits or technical measurements. Other endpoint denominators differ. No imputation was reported; complete-series exclusion is consistent with the counts and degrees of freedom but is not an explicitly stated analysis rule.

The parallel design avoids crossover carryover. It used 50 eccentric contractions and measured strength relative to **day-five pre-exercise values, after four supplementation days and the morning drink**. This differs from the earlier pre-supplementation baseline. The two percentage outcomes cannot be treated as an identical estimand.

Average strength losses were 14.6% with cherry, 19.4% with pomegranate and 17.3% with control. The **three-group** tests were P=.77 for group and P=.91 for group×time. The arithmetic cherry–control difference is 2.7 percentage points less loss, without a published pairwise P value or confidence interval. These omnibus tests are not pairwise evidence or an equivalence test. The challenge produced a pooled 26.8% decrement at 24 hours, so there was a measurable deficit to recover from.

Soreness, motion and CK treatment tests were also nonsignificant. Excluding six CK hyper-responders in an exploratory analysis did not reveal a strength effect; its remaining endpoint counts were not supplied. Small groups, missing assays and absent pairwise intervals leave smaller effects unresolved. A unique prospective primary endpoint, power calculation and public protocol were not verified. The source's unexplained baseline-strength unit and other reporting inconsistencies remain in the structured evidence; no absolute-force inference is reconstructed from them.

## Competitiveness and next evidence

This is an actual controlled functional signal for a specified blend, with relevant counterevidence from a different preparation. It earns compensation-oriented credit without a BUBR1 assay. The short healthy-exercise setting, proprietary composition, uncertain crossover effects and lack of durable disease outcomes prevent ranking clinical benefit against niacin or apitegromab. Those nominees also lack demonstrated MVA rescue, so that gap is not imposed only on challengers. Neither nomination changes here.

Advancement would require an identifiable preparation or appropriately characterized metabolites, justified measured exposure, and useful absolute muscle output with preservation of renewable reserve and chromosome fidelity in relevant affected/corrected models. Functional BUBR1 mediation is additionally required only for a restoration claim. Current retail equivalence and approved-drug submission eligibility remain unestablished.

Cherrypharm funded the 2006 trial, and each author disclosed 2.5% company equity. The later study was university-funded, with a separately disclosed Cherry Marketing Institute gout-study grant. Neither source supplies systematic safety evidence sufficient for a pediatric, cancer-context or long-term safety claim. The withdrawals and missed visits must not be recast as demonstrated supplement toxicity or proof of no adverse events.

A [Staud commentary](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17092438/) is indexed on the earlier trial, but lawful retrieval provided only subscription-preview metadata and references. Its scientific content and any directly associated response remain unreviewed; its implications are unknown. No efficacy inference or extra study credit is assigned from it.
